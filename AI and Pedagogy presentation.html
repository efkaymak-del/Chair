Level 1 · The Point

AI didn't replace anything here.

It built infrastructure that would otherwise require a web developer and weeks of work. The instructor provides content expertise and learning design. The AI provides technical execution. The entire app runs client-side — no server, no database, no API keys. It's hosted free on GitHub Pages.

Level 2 · Skills Scaffolding

PSIR220 — Research Design

Students struggle with methodology. They can describe theories but can't operationalize them into research. The gap between "I understand process tracing" and "I can do process tracing" is enormous.

Solution: A paradigm-specific research toolkit — every tool walks students through a thinking process, not just content recall.

⚔️ Strategist

Positivist track · KKV · Hypotheses → observable implications → evidence → rival explanation → cautious verdict

📖 Storyteller

Interpretivist track · George & Bennett · Starting conditions → causal chain links → breaks → alternative pathway → verdict

🔥 Disruptor

Critical track · Milliken · Corpus → discursive patterns → productivity & foreclosure → dominant account → positionality → revelation

Level 3 · Experiential Simulation

PSIR172 — Applied Skills: Model United Nations

MUN requires country research, speech writing, negotiation strategy, and resolution drafting. Students often arrive unprepared and flounder. The gap between "I know about refugees" and "I can represent Jordan's position on refugee burden-sharing" is vast.

Solution: A complete MUN ecosystem — from preparation through simulation to resolution drafting.

Level 3 · The Key Move

"Research goal: not 'know everything about the country,' but 'know enough to act like its delegation in this committee.'"

The MUN preparation workshop explicitly teaches the difference between a weak approach (capital city, population, flag, random history) and a strong approach (role on the issue, pressures, likely support, likely opposition, acceptable wording). AI built the scaffolding for an exercise that is fundamentally about doing, not knowing.

Level 4 · Philosophical Inquiry

PSIR308 — Contemporary Political Thought

Political theory is often taught as lecture → reading → essay. Students can define theories but can't use them as analytical instruments. They know what Rawls said but not what happens when Fanon interrogates him.

Solution: Scenario-based philosophical simulations where students apply theoretical lenses to dilemmas — and discover their own commitments.

Level 4 · Philosophical Inquiry

The PSIR308 Simulations

Feminism App

10 social arrangements examined through 4 lenses: liberal feminism, radical feminism (MacKinnon), socialist feminism, justice-in-the-family (Okin). Students choose which lens is most revealing — their pattern reveals their theoretical intuitions.

Communitarianism App

8 dilemmas: individual autonomy vs. communal belonging. Choose Liberal, Communitarian, or Compromise for each. Your pattern emerges.

Pre-Political Simulation

Beauvoir & Fanon. 8 scenarios — liberal analysis vs. pre-political critique. Generates a profile: Radical Deconstructionist, Critical Liberal, Institutional Pragmatist, or Situational Thinker.

Level 4 · The Live Classroom

Critical Theory Workshop

Students arrive with their pre-political simulation profiles. The instructor-led workshop app structures the live session:

Block 1 — Map the Room

Students submit profiles. Live distribution appears on screen. "Who shifted? Who was consistent? What drove your pattern?"

Block 2 — Theories on Trial

Rawls, Nozick, MacKinnon. Beauvoir and Fanon are the prosecutors. Real-time voting: Survived / Needs revision / Fundamentally broken.

Block 3 — The Course Itself

Scenario 8. "You are studying Beauvoir and Fanon in English, at a university modelled on Western higher education, in a territory shaped by colonialism and partition."

Level 4 · Scenario 8
Fanon said that the university is the most refined instrument of colonial subject-construction.

Is this course an act of liberation or an act of assimilation — or both? — PSIR 308 Critical Theory Workshop, Scenario 8

The technology does the logistics — collecting votes, displaying distributions, sequencing blocks. The intellectual work is irreducibly human and deeply Socratic. AI built the vehicle. The pedagogy requires being in the room together.

Act 4 · Policy as Enabler

Faculty AI Policy — Version 1.0

Effective Academic Year 2026–2027 · For review and adoption by the Faculty Board

Grounded in the EU Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI (High-Level Expert Group, 2019). This is distinctive: most university AI policies are ad hoc rules. Ours is anchored in an established, principled ethical framework — the same framework the EU uses for AI regulation.

The framework rests on four ethical imperatives: respect for human autonomy, prevention of harm, fairness, and explicability. From these, seven operational requirements.

Act 4 · The Seven Principles

EU Trustworthy AI → Student Academic Work

Principle
What it means for students
1. Human agency & oversight
You remain the author and thinker. AI may assist, but your judgment directs and checks every output.
2. Technical robustness
AI outputs can be wrong or fabricated. Verify facts, data, and citations before relying on them.
3. Privacy & data governance
Do not enter confidential or personal data into AI tools without authorization.
4. Transparency
Disclose when and how you used AI. Your work must be traceable and honestly attributed.
5. Diversity & fairness
Be alert to bias in AI outputs. Misuse for unfair advantage is prohibited.
6. Societal well-being
Use AI responsibly, mindful of its wider ethical, social, and resource costs.
7. Accountability
You are fully responsible for everything you submit, including any AI-assisted content.
Act 4 · The Three-Tier Taxonomy

Permitted · Authorized Only · Prohibited

Tier
Examples
Requirement
✓ Permitted
Brainstorming, explaining concepts, grammar/style, summarizing readings, generating practice questions, debugging your own code
Allowed unless instructor says otherwise; disclose in AI-use statement
⚠ Authorized only
Drafting text sections, generating data/figures, translating substantial passages, producing code as a deliverable
Only when course/assignment explicitly allows it; disclose fully
✕ Prohibited
Submitting AI-generated work as your own, using AI in closed exams, fabricating data/sources, bypassing the learning objective
Not allowed; treated as academic misconduct
Act 4 · The Disclosure Requirement

The Model Statement

"In preparing this work I used [tool name] to [brief description, e.g. brainstorm ideas / check grammar / explain a concept]. I reviewed and verified all output, and I take full responsibility for the content of this submission."

This is additive, not punitive. It normalizes transparency. It makes the invisible visible. And it gives instructors a foundation for conversation rather than accusation.

Act 4 · Syllabus Integration

The Provisional Clause

Per Department Council Karar 6 (14 September 2026):

Syllabi for Fall 2026–2027 go to students the week of 22 September with three components finalized: (a) learning outcomes in active verbs, (b) assessment items mapped to programme learning outcomes, (c) rebalanced assessment weights. The AI use clause is marked as provisional (geçici). Once the Faculty Board formalizes the policy, instructors update only that clause within one week.

This buys time without leaving a vacuum. Students know from day one that AI rules exist and are coming; instructors aren't forced to finalize before the institutional position is clear.

Act 4 · The Connection

Policy Enables Practice

Without a clear policy, colleagues are paralyzed — some ban AI entirely, others ignore it. A principled framework liberates everyone to experiment purposefully.

The same "human agency and oversight" principle that governs student use is what the instructor practiced in every one of those 60 apps: AI builds, the instructor designs and decides.
